# DIRECTOR'S VISION

Translate the supplied story into a coherent audience experience: what viewers notice, understand, anticipate, and feel; how characters behave; how space, light, camera, editing, and sound express the drama.

Work at the stage between the screenplay and detailed storyboarding. Make concrete directing decisions, explain their dramatic purpose, and give downstream production work enough information to preserve them. Keep this skill self-contained and usable without other installed skills.

## 1. Establish scope and source authority

1. Identify the requested project, source version, medium, and scope: complete film, episode, sequence, scene, or revision. Read the supplied material for that scope. For a complete-work request, inspect the complete source before claiming complete coverage. For a selected scene, read relevant adjacent material and constraints without importing unrelated projects.
2. Reuse stated runtime, aspect ratio, audience, genre, language, visual references, budget constraints, approved identities, and available assets. Do not ask again for information already supplied.
3. If no source is available, ask once for the screenplay, scene, treatment, or idea. If only an idea or excerpt is provided, create a provisional vision at that resolution; identify the supplied boundary. Do not claim to have read an unseen book or invent an approved screenplay.
4. Make reversible creative choices and identify them as proposals. Ask only when a missing fact or conflicting instruction would materially change the requested work. Continue unaffected work while identifying the conflict.
5. Follow current explicit user instructions. Preserve the newest approved story and design decisions within the requested scope. If two approved sources conflict, state the conflict and its consequences instead of silently choosing a new canon.
6. Keep source facts, character beliefs, production proposals, and unresolved information distinct. A character's belief is not objective truth. A proposed image is not proof of a source event. An accepted portrait governs identity when the user has designated it as authoritative.

Record a compact project header: title; source and version; covered scene/page/chapter range; medium; target runtime if known; aspect ratio if known; requested tone; approved constraints; assumptions. Use `unspecified` for unknown facts rather than fabricated precision.

Use workflow states `draft`, `refine`, or `final` and decision states `proposed`, `selected`, `approved`, or `superseded` where they help. Do not promote your own proposals to approved decisions.

## 2. Select the requested output depth

- **Vision — default:** Deliver the director's statement, visual and performance rules, a direction map covering the supplied scope, selected signature moments, and a storyboard/animatic handoff. Include one compact worked sequence treatment when the supplied material supports it. Do not expand every scene into a full shot list without a request for that depth.
- **Detailed sequence:** Extend the requested scene or sequence into blocking, beat-by-beat shot planning, storyboard panel instructions, and provisional animatic timing.
- **Full breakdown:** Apply the detailed sequence format across all requested scenes. Keep a coverage index and preserve scene IDs. If the work spans messages, retain a clear continuation point and finish the requested scope; do not present a sample as a complete film.
- **Revision:** Change the requested directing decisions, preserve everything else, and identify the affected downstream scenes, shots, assets, or audio cues.

Choose the mode from the user's request without requiring a menu. Do not make the user complete a questionnaire to receive a first useful draft.

## 3. Find the dramatic basis of the vision

Extract the protagonist's drive, the central pressure, the principal relationships, the emotional change, the audience's intended experience, and the story's tonal boundaries.

Write a director's statement of approximately 150–300 words when the scope warrants it. Make it specific to this story. Explain:

- Why this story deserves this approach and whose experience leads it.
- What the audience should feel at the beginning, at the key turn, and at the end.
- How the visible world and performances express the theme.
- Which visual contrast or recurring image develops with the characters.
- How spectacle, intimacy, suspense, and humour relate where those tones actually belong.

Turn abstract adjectives into observable decisions. For example, express isolation through a character's placement, separation from others, sound perspective, or changing frame size. Avoid pages of interchangeable claims such as "epic, cinematic, immersive, emotional."

Preserve the source's events, character motivations, reveal order, dialogue, and ending unless the user authorizes changes. Identify any proposed story addition and its downstream effect separately. Do not invent extra scenes, powers, crowds, romances, quips, or cliffhangers merely to make the film appear larger.

## 4. Translate references into an original approach

Treat named films as references for identifiable craft choices. Build a small reference map: desired quality → technique → adaptation to this story → boundary or exception. Use only the aspects the user wants.

When Iron Man is the reference, consider these creative lenses where appropriate:

- **Personality under pressure:** Let choices, competence, flaws, reactions, and relationships shape the spectacle.
- **Preparation and payoff:** Establish the object, ability, rule, or vulnerability that makes a later event understandable.
- **Tactile detail:** Show mechanisms, contact, material response, weight, recovery, and consequences.
- **Controlled reveals:** Arrange sound, partial information, recognition, and scale to build anticipation.
- **Readable action:** Establish the objective, threat, route, and changing advantage.
- **Human aftermath:** Allow the audience to register relief, cost, closeness, doubt, or a deserved comic beat.

Use these as optional directing heuristics, not a formula or a claim that every film in the series uses the same style. Preserve the new story's genre and identity. Do not automatically import superhero suits, HUDs, red-and-gold colours, technological lore, or franchise plot beats.

When asked for "more cinematic" or "cooler shots," increase specificity, visual progression, composition, staging, and contrast. Select moments where ambitious camera work improves discovery or emotion. Do not equate quality with continuous camera movement, permanent shallow focus, slow motion, extra explosions, or a fixed shot quota.

Do not fabricate film timecodes, lens data, filmmakers' intentions, quotations, or production facts. Verify factual reference analysis with available primary sources when needed; label your own interpretations as interpretations.

## 5. Establish the film's visual and sonic language

Build a concise rule table with columns `Element | Story purpose | Proposed rule | Development or exception`. Cover the relevant elements below with concrete choices.

### Perspective and composition

Define whose knowledge guides each sequence and when viewers know more or less than a character. Specify the use of shared frames, singles, foreground layers, depth, negative space, silhouettes, and character size within the frame. Show how those choices evolve with relationships or power.

### Camera and lens character

Set the baseline camera behaviour and the dramatic events that justify changing it. Distinguish a camera move from subject movement and from an edit. State what a move reveals, follows, withholds, or changes.

Describe camera height, viewing distance, field of view, focus priorities, and perspective clearly. Treat focal lengths as provisional references unless sensor or film format is specified. Do not claim that naming a lens or camera in an AI prompt guarantees its optics. Preserve enough depth of field to read action and relationships when needed.

For an elaborate moving shot, define the start, path, end, attention changes, and a simpler coverage alternative. Use a deliberate re-establishing view or an on-screen change when crossing the action axis or reversing screen direction.

### Light, colour, materials, and world

Identify motivated light sources, direction, contrast, time of day, weather, and how special effects illuminate nearby surfaces. Assign a palette by story function and location; allow coherent variations across day, night, interiors, and emotional states.

Define useful material and environmental rules: wear, reflections, fabric behaviour, scale, practical clutter, atmosphere, and persistent damage. Preserve approved character, wardrobe, location, and prop designs. A new lighting situation is not permission to redesign an identity or set.

### Editing and time

Describe the rhythm of anticipation, action, thought, and aftermath. Choose cuts for information, reactions, movement, contrast, or sound. Specify when sustained observation or an unbroken shared frame is valuable.

Let duration follow dialogue, performance, physical action, and audience comprehension. Do not impose 2–5-second shots, equal scene lengths, a universal hook schedule, or arbitrary episode runtimes. Separate final edit duration from the duration of a generated source clip.

### Sound and music

Define the sound perspective, meaningful offscreen sounds, recurring motifs, ambience, silence, impacts, and transitions. State when music enters, recedes, or leaves room for performance. Include sounds that prepare a reveal before its source appears.

For speaking scenes, plan intention, pauses, breath, and listener reactions. Use scratch dialogue to test pacing; establish the selected voice timing before final audio-driven lip-sync. Do not specify unsupported provider markup or claim audio has been produced when only notes exist.

## 6. Direct characters and tonal changes

For each principal character within scope, specify the immediate objective, the obstacle, the relationship to the listener, the tactic, and the observable adjustment when a beat turns.

Direct playable behaviour: attempts to reassure, conceal, challenge, distract, persuade, protect, or reconnect. Connect that action to gaze, posture, distance, touch, hands, breath, vocal pace, pauses, and restrained facial changes. Avoid a list of unrelated emotion adjectives or constant gestures.

Plan listening as well as speaking. Show who notices a change and what they do with that information. Keep movement, voice, and expression physically compatible.

Adapt to the actual genre:

- **Action:** Plan preparation, commitment, contact, displacement, recovery, and consequence; show how tactics change under pressure.
- **Fantasy or science fiction:** Make effects obey the established world rules, scale, limitations, and cost. Let surrounding materials, light, and people react consistently.
- **Romance:** Develop proximity, avoidance, shared attention, invitations, responses, and changes in trust. Preserve the characters' established intentions and boundaries.
- **Comedy:** Build setup, recognition, timing, reaction, and consequence. Let humour arise from character and situation. Protect grief, danger, and sincerity when a joke would undercut the intended scene.
- **Suspense or horror:** Control information, offscreen space, anticipation, and release. Preserve clues and the intended reveal order.
- **Quiet drama:** Give stillness, hesitation, ordinary tasks, and changing attention room to carry the scene. Do not add spectacle merely because a reference contains action.

## 7. Map sequences, scenes, and physical continuity

Use existing scene IDs. If the source lacks them, assign stable IDs and explain that they are production labels. Do not silently renumber approved material during revisions.

Create a direction map covering every scene in the requested, available scope:

`Scene ID | Source anchor | Objective and obstacle | Entry → turn → exit | Audience knowledge/feeling | Visual and performance approach | Sound/transition | Continuity dependencies`

Group scenes into sequences where useful while retaining their individual IDs. For an idea without established scenes, use clearly labelled proposed units rather than pretending the table represents a completed screenplay.

For each scene requiring detailed treatment, establish the physical arrangement before camera design:

- Set boundaries, landmarks, entrances, exits, obstacles, and relevant distances; mark invented dimensions as design estimates.
- Character starting positions, orientation, routes, stopping points, gaze targets, and destinations.
- The action axis and intended screen direction; distinguish world coordinates from camera-relative left/right.
- Important objects, who holds them, anatomical hand used, contact changes, and visibility.
- Light direction, weather, costume state, wounds, debris, damage, and other persistent conditions relevant to the scene.

Track state at meaningful beats and cuts. Specify the connective action, established ellipsis, or motivated re-establishing view when a position or state changes. Do not use a cut to conceal an unexplained teleport, a reversed doorway, a resized object, or a disappearing injury.

If a hidden object is discovered, establish what concealed it and how that concealment changes. If a prop is dropped or exchanged, preserve where it goes and when possession changes. Use anatomical left/right for body features and hands, with viewer-left/right added when necessary.

## 8. Design signature moments and useful coverage

Choose signature moments at meaningful discoveries, reversals, commitments, relationship changes, or consequences. Match their number and complexity to the story. A held reaction or shared frame can be a signature moment.

For each selected moment, provide:

1. **Anchor:** Scene ID and source beat.
2. **Audience effect:** The information or emotion it delivers.
3. **Preparation:** What the audience must already know and what earlier shot or sound establishes it.
4. **Staging:** Character, prop, environment, and attention changes.
5. **Camera:** Framing, position, movement, focus, and start/end compositions.
6. **Event and consequence:** The decisive action, reaction, and continuing physical or emotional state.
7. **Sound:** The cue, silence, dialogue, or transition that shapes the moment.
8. **Production route:** Required assets, a focused test for a difficult interaction, and a simpler alternative that preserves meaning.

Select from a varied vocabulary when useful: foreground reveals, detail-to-scale transitions, low lateral tracking, tactical overheads, reflection reveals, focus changes, held wide action, moving shared frames, still close-ups, and sound-led transitions. Do not cycle through this list mechanically.

Plan coverage around editorial needs: a readable spatial view where needed, key interactions, reactions, meaningful inserts, and alternate coverage of fragile moments. Avoid redundant angles with no story or editing purpose. Keep faces and performances whenever they carry the drama.

## 9. Build the requested storyboard and animatic handoff

For detailed work, use one shot record per planned continuous camera view. A shot can require several storyboard panels. Distinguish `SCENE`, `SHOT`, `PANEL`, `ASSET`, and `CLIP`; several panels can describe one shot, and several generated clips may supply one edited shot.

Use this record, omitting only genuinely irrelevant fields:

```yaml
shot_id: SC03_SH012
source_anchor: scene and exact beat or dialogue anchor
decision_status: proposed
purpose: audience information or emotion
planned_edit_duration_seconds: estimate, range, or unspecified
timing_basis: dialogue read, action estimate, or reviewed animatic
framing_and_camera: position, height, view, focus, movement path, end frame
opening_state: character positions, orientation, gaze, hands, props, environment
performance_and_action: ordered actions and reactions, with beat turns
dialogue_and_sound: exact source lines, performance notes, ambience, cues
closing_state: resulting positions, contact, possession, damage, gaze, emotion
continuity_to_next: what matches, what changes, and how the change is explained
cut_or_transition: reason and intended connection
storyboard_panels: key compositions or action stages; panel IDs
required_assets: approved references, versions, and missing assets
production_notes: testable difficulty and simpler coverage option
```

Represent preparation, contact, landing, recovery, or changing camera position with enough panels to explain them. Distinguish designed timing from measured timing. For an animatic plan, state time ranges, whether shots run sequentially, and which sounds overlap. Sum planned shot durations and show any gap against the target runtime; do not fill a shortfall with arbitrary slow motion or empty shots.

Specify the animatic review questions: geography, reveal timing, dialogue space, physical plausibility, reaction time, emotional progression, comedy timing, and useful coverage. Revise the smallest failed element before expanding final production.

Call a text table an **animatic plan**. Call an actual edited, playable audiovisual artifact an **animatic** only after it has been created. Apply the same distinction to storyboard instructions versus generated boards, written prompts versus images, and animation directions versus finished clips.

## 10. Adapt the production handoff to the medium

For AI filmmaking, preserve approved identity, costume, location, prop, scale, lighting, and shot references with stable IDs and versions. Record missing assets honestly; never mark a reference as generated or approved because a filename was proposed.

Separate creative intention from provider controls. Check current capabilities only when a specific tool, setting, model limit, price, or generation route is needed. Do not hardcode unlimited credits, free tiers, model versions, clip lengths, resolution support, reference limits, or guaranteed lip-sync.

Test difficult contact, multiple characters, fast action, changing viewpoints, or complex effects with a small representative portion. Where execution is uncertain, propose alternate coverage or a layered production route. Preserve cause and effect when splitting action across clips. Select a coherent performance and edit rather than assuming every requested detail will survive generation.

For live action or rigged animation, translate the same creative decisions into appropriate blocking, camera, lighting, performance, and editorial requirements. Do not impose AI-generation restrictions on other media.

Default to the requested written directing package. Generate images, video, audio, or an edited animatic only when that work is requested and the relevant tools are available. Treat output status precisely: `planned`, `created`, `reviewed`, or `approved by user`. Do not imply publishing, purchases, or extra installations are included in a directing request.

## 12. Deliver and check the package

Use the user's language for the directing document. Use English for generation prompts only when requested or required by the selected workflow. Write concrete, usable decisions; scale detail to the supplied material and requested mode.

For the default vision, deliver these sections:

1. **Project basis and scope** — source, constraints, known decisions, and assumptions.
2. **Director's statement** — the story-specific intention and audience journey.
3. **Visual and sonic language** — practical rules and their development.
4. **Character and tonal direction** — performance, relationships, and tonal boundaries.
5. **Scene/sequence direction map** — complete coverage of the available requested scope.
6. **Signature moments** — specific visual ideas with motivation, preparation, and aftermath.
7. **Worked sequence and production handoff** — include the requested level of blocking, shots, panels, timing, assets, and tests.
8. **Open decisions and next deliverable** — only material unknowns and the next concrete output.

For a narrow task or revision, deliver only the relevant sections and affected handoff records. Avoid repeating unchanged project bibles. In a revision, include `preserved`, `changed`, and `downstream effects` without inventing new approval status.

Before delivery, check:

- Source coverage and scene IDs match the stated scope; unread material is identified.
- Approved events, identities, ages, relationships, world rules, and reveal order remain intact.
- Proposals and assumptions are distinguishable from source facts and user approvals.
- Each scene has a purpose and a meaningful change appropriate to the source.
- Camera choices, staging, sound, and performance support the intended audience experience.
- Physical routes, scale, contact, gaze, possession, light, and damage connect across relevant cuts.
- Shot lengths allow the action and speech; stated sequential timings add up.
- Spectacle has preparation and consequence; human reactions remain present.
- Every claimed image, clip, voice, storyboard, or animatic actually exists in the stated form.
- The next stage can proceed from the handoff without inventing missing identity or geography.

Resolve correctable issues before presenting the package. Report specific limitations without assigning a fabricated cinematic-quality score or promising a studio-level result from a prompt alone.
